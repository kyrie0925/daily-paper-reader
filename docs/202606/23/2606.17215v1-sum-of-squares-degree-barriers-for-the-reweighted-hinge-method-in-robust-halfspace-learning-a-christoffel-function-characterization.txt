# Sum-of-Squares Degree Barriers for the Reweighted-Hinge Method in Robust Halfspace Learning: A Christoffel-Function Characterization

University of New South Wales

Abstract 

A certificate that removes outliers sees the data only through its low-degree moments, and an adversary exploits exactly this, hiding corruption where the clean data already looks typical, in the blind spot no bounded-degree test resolves. That blind spot turns out to have an exact size: the Christoffel function of the clean marginal, the very quantity modern data analysis thresholds to detect 

outliers, here read from the adversary’s side as the corruption a bounded-degree certificate cannot 

remove . We turn this inversion into the organizing principle of the reweighted-hinge approach to robustly learning γ-margin halfspaces under malicious noise (Shen, 2025; Zeng and Shen, 2025): the governing resource is the Sum-of-Squares degree of the outlier-removal certificate, and the resolution principle states that the maximal corruption mass which can hide at a center c from a degree-2 t

certificate is exactly the Christoffel function λt+1(c) of the clean marginal. Three consequences follow, all against the certificate method (not information-theoretic). A margin–degree tradeoff : certifying the dense pancake to error ϵ costs SoS degree Ω(log (1/ϵ )) or margin Ω(plog (1/ϵ )/pd), explaining why the log (1/ϵ ) margin Shen (2025) records is forced , with a weighted-Chebyshev reduction making the threshold 2 t = Θ(( |c|/s)2) tight modulo one classical weighted-extremal estimate. A degree-2

outlier barrier : the resolution principle realized as an explicit instance on which degree 2 is stuck at η1/2 while degree 4 escapes, locating the method’s small breakdown rate in the degree, not the analysis. And a degree-2t algorithm tracing the frontier η1−1/2t (recovering Shen (2025) at t = 1), whose gain is an explicit constant, capped by the pancake density and shown unimprovable by the degree-2 barrier.

The problem is to learn a halfspace efficiently when a constant fraction of the training data has been corrupted by an adversary. In the malicious-noise model of Kearns and Li (1993), an adversary observes the clean sample, then replaces an η-fraction of the (instance, label) pairs by arbitrary points of its choosing, with full knowledge of the learner and the clean distribution; the goal is to output ˆw whose error on the clean inliers is at most ϵ. This is the strongest of the standard corruption models, and the central question is how large a noise rate η a polynomial-time learner can tolerate. One influential line of work answers this through a single algorithmic device: hinge-loss minimization, later reweighted to suppress outliers. Talwar (2020) observed that plain hinge-loss minimization already resists noise when the clean marginal places dense mass in a band (a “pancake”) around every point’s projection onto the separating direction. Shen (2025) turned this into a constant-noise-rate guarantee for log-concave-mixture marginals by reweighting the hinge: the weights come from a degree-2 outlier-removal program (a variance constraint), and the analysis controls the dirty contribution to the hinge gradient through the quantity LSN (q ◦ SD) = sup ∥w∥≤ 1

The tolerable rate is tiny ( η0 = 2−32 in the published version), and Shen (2025) notes it “cannot be made close to the optimal breakdown point of 12 due to inherent limitations of our approach”; and the required margin carries an extra logarithmic factor, γ = Ω(log (1/ϵ )/pd), a “logarithmic dependence on 1 /ϵ that appears less natural” than the Ω(1/pd) margin one would hope for. The same two features recur in the sparse follow-up of Zeng and Shen (2025). The margin’s dependence on ϵ is a chicken-and-egg problem. A margin is a fixed property of the data distribution, whereas the accuracy ϵ is the learner’s target, set at will once the problem is in hand; requiring γ = Ω(log (1/ϵ )/pd) couples the two backwards, demanding better-separated data exactly when the learner asks for smaller error, a property the data cannot supply on request. One would rather fix the geometry once and buy accuracy with computation, not with margin. This paper explains both limitations. We argue that they are not artifacts of a particular constant or a loose inequality, but consequences of a single resource that governs the whole approach: the polynomial, equivalently Sum-of-Squares (SoS), degree of the outlier-removal certificate. The reweighted-hinge method needs two distributional facts about the clean marginal, and both are supplied by certificates that read off only the low-order moments: a lower bound on the mass of the dense pancake (so the hinge has signal), and an upper bound on a reweighted moment of the dirty points (so the outlier program can shrink LSN ). Each is a degree-2 t SoS proof from the first 2 t moments. We show that the degree these proofs require is exactly what controls the margin factor and the noise rate, and that Shen’s degree-2 program sits at a corner of a tight frontier. We frame the contribution, throughout, in terms of degree: degree is to this method what the approximate degree of a predicate is to the polynomial method in algorithm design – the parameter that simultaneously buys the guarantee and bounds it. Our two lower bounds are degree barriers; our algorithm is a degree-2 t program that traces the frontier the barriers cut out. As with any barrier of this kind, the scope is essential and we state it up front: the lower bounds are against the moment /SoS-certificate method – any procedure whose only distributional input is the degree-≤ 2t certified moments of the clean marginal. They are not information-theoretic, and they are not unconditional Statistical-Query hardness. The margin–degree barrier strengthens, per direction, to a genuine degree-2 t Sum-of-Squares lower bound (Remark 3.1); lifting it to the full d-dimensional reweighting program jointly is open for 3t ≥ 2 (Section 5.2). This is precisely the class of methods to which Shen’s “our approach” belongs, which is why the barriers explain that line’s admitted limits rather than contradicting them.

1.1 Technical overview 

Three ideas drive the results; we sketch each before the formal proofs in Sections 3 and 4 and the appendix. All three are facets of the same Christoffel-function picture (Section 3): Gauss quadrature locates where the Christoffel function resolves a band, the moment cloak realizes the smallest unresolved spike, and the degree-2 t Hölder bound is that detector run as a certificate. 

(a) Gauss quadrature beats the moment problem. A degree-2 t certificate of band mass is, after a minorant reduction, a polynomial p of degree ≤ 2t with p ≤ 1B together with an SoS proof, from the moments alone, that R p ≥ ρ. Because the proof uses only m0, . . . , m2t , its conclusion must hold for every 

nonnegative measure matching those moments. Gauss quadrature produces one such measure explicitly: the (t + 1)-point measure on the roots of the degree-(t + 1) orthogonal polynomial of μ reproduces all moments up to order 2 t + 1, and it is supported on just t + 1 atoms. If the band B contains no quadrature node, this witness assigns it mass zero, so the best certifiable value is zero and no degree-2 t certificate can exist. A band stays node-free as long as it lies beyond the largest root, which sits at Θ(spt) for sub-Gaussian weights and Θ(s t ) for sub-exponential ones. An off-center band at distance |c| is thus invisible below degree (|c|/s)2, the source of the tradeoff. The largest-root bound is elementary in the two cases we name (Jacobi-matrix eigenvalues with Gershgorin for the sub-Gaussian 2 spt + 1 and sub-exponential O(st ) endpoints); the fully general log-concave exponent is the one place we invoke a black box, the exponential-weight asymptotics of Levin and Lubinsky (2001), and we flag it. 6(b) The moment-cloaked spike. Scope and open questions. Two cautions are built into the results above and we restate them together. The barriers are method-specific : they rule out the degree-2 t moment /SoS-certificate family, not all efficient algorithms, and not learners outside the one-shot certificate model (an iterative, localization-style filter sidesteps one-shot certification and is not covered). And the algorithm’s gain is modest : it removes the degree-2 exponent loss but stays capped by ρ/ 4 and does not march toward 1 /2. Several questions are left open, in increasing order of reach. The breakdown floor is rate-tight but not threshold-tight; the conjectured margin-dependent rate η⋆ = Θ(γ/σ ) needs a multi-point or band-mass argument beyond two points. The degree barrier is a lower bound; a matching upper certificate of degree O(( |c|/s)2)

would make the tradeoff exactly 2 t = Θ(( |c|/s)2). The general-log-concave largest-root bound still rests on Levin and Lubinsky (2001); removing that black box would make the barrier self-contained. And whether an iterative localization scheme provably escapes the margin–degree barrier (by leaving the one-shot certificate model the barrier scopes) is the question whose answer would change the picture rather than refine it. 

1.2 Related work 

The hinge approach we analyze originates with Talwar (2020) and is sharpened by reweighting in Shen (2025), then Zeng and Shen (2025). Klivans et al. (2025) reach error 2 η + ϵ for halfspaces under contamination, but for Gaussian marginals, without a margin, and by iterative filtering rather than a moment certificate, so they are a motivation for the certificate model and not subsumed by it. The SoS 7machinery we import is from robust statistics (Diakonikolas et al., 2024; Hopkins and Li, 2018; Klivans et al., 2018; Kothari et al., 2018); we use it to certify a moment upper bound and, for the barrier, to scope the certificate model precisely. The dual direction (certifiable anti -concentration, an SoS lower bound on slab mass (Bakshi et al., 2024)) is exactly what a degree-2 t band-mass certificate would need and exactly what our Theorem 4.1 shows the low-order moments cannot supply. Lower bounds for nasty and agnostic halfspaces (Diakonikolas et al., 2018, 2020) address different noise models and do not give a degree barrier for LSN . The orthogonal-polynomial facts we use are classical (Gautschi, 2004; Kriecherbauer and McLaughlin, 1999; Levin and Lubinsky, 2001; Szeg˝ o, 1939), as are the log-concave (Brascamp and Lieb, 1976; Lovász and Vempala, 2007) and malicious-noise (Kearns and Li, 1993) ingredients. 

# 2 Preliminaries 

This section fixes the noise model, the distributional assumptions, the reweighted-hinge program whose dirty gradient we control, and the two algebraic objects that the rest of the paper turns on: the dense pancake and the degree-2 t moment certificate of its band mass. The reader should leave with one picture in mind. Outlier removal in this line of work is a polynomial certificate of a band-mass lower bound, its degree is the resource, and the distributional input that powers it is a degree-2 t certifiable moment bound (Lemma 4.13); every barrier we prove is a lower bound on that degree.

# 3 The Christoffel function and the certificate ceiling 

Start from the adversary’s vantage point. A degree-2 t outlier-removal certificate reads the clean marginal only through its first 2 t moments, so a corruption is invisible to it exactly when some nonnegative measure that still carries the corruption matches those moments. One quantity then decides everything: among all measures sharing the clean marginal’s first 2 t moments, how much mass can be piled at a single center c? A rate-η corruption planted at c stays beyond the certificate’s reach for as long as that maximum exceeds η.We did not have to invent this maximum. The most mass a moment-matching measure can place at 

c is exactly the Christoffel function λt+1(c) of the marginal (Fact 3.2), the reciprocal of the Christoffel– Darboux kernel on its diagonal. The certificate model thus hands us the Christoffel function unbidden, as the resolution threshold of its own moment cone; the resolution principle (Proposition 3.3) makes the equivalence exact. Everything below is a reading of where λt+1(c) falls relative to the corruption it must resolve: it is large at a tail center for small degree (a corruption hides), it collapses past the node edge (a band is forced empty), and it crosses η at a computable order (the corruption is exposed). The learning consequences of these three readings are drawn in Section 4. Two literatures meet here. On one side, the certificate ceiling is an instance of the truncated moment problem (Curto and Fialkow, 1991; Dette and Studden, 1997) and of the moment–SOS hierarchy (Lasserre, 2021). On the other, the same Christoffel function is, in modern data analysis, exactly the moment-based outlier detector (Beckermann et al., 2021; Lasserre and Pauwels, 2019), run (as we will see) with the opposite sign. We quantify the learning-theoretic limits of that detector. The three figures of this section are one Christoffel curve seen from three distances: a far band swept empty past the node edge (Figure 2), the curve crossing the corruption rate η at a computable center (Figure 3), and the same curve carrying the detector’s meaning in reverse (Figure 1).

3.3 The degree of robustness is the Christoffel degree 

Proposition 3.3 is the lower-bound counterpart of a tool already in use. Lasserre and Pauwels (2019) and Beckermann et al. (2021) detect outliers and recover supports by thresholding the empirical Christoffel 14 function: a point x is flagged when λn(x) (built from the empirical moments) is small, i.e. when degree-n

moments place x outside the bulk. Proposition 3.3 says that this is exactly the mechanism, and the only mechanism, available to a degree-2 t outlier-removal program: it can down-weight a planted corruption at 

c if and only if the order-(t+1) Christoffel function already resolves it, λt+1(c) < η . The reweighted-hinge programs of Shen (2025); Talwar (2020) run this detector at degree 2; raising the hinge program to degree 2 t is raising the Christoffel detector to order t+1. We can now state the law the framework yields, the degree of robustness : the SoS degree at which a certificate first tolerates corruption rate η at margin scale equals the Christoffel order that resolves a mass-η cluster at the η-tail of the marginal, 

t⋆(η) = min  t : λt+1(σ/ pη) < η .It is a property of μ and the order, which is why the resource is degree and not sample size: more samples sharpen the empirical Christoffel function toward its population limit, but the population value at the planted center is fixed by the marginal and the order, so an infinite sample at fixed degree buys nothing against a hidden spike. The only knob that lowers λt+1(c) past η is the order itself. The degree-2 t

algorithm of Section 4 is this law made operational. 

Relationship to the Christoffel-function program of Lasserre, Pauwels, and Putinar. The Christoffel function is by now a central object in moment-based data analysis (Lasserre and Pauwels, 2019; Lasserre et al., 2022; Pauwels and Lasserre, 2016; Pauwels et al., 2021), where the empirical Christoffel function serves as a detector : a large value λn(x) marks x as typical, a small value flags it as an outlier, and sublevel sets recover the shape of a point cloud (Beckermann et al., 2021; Pauwels and Lasserre, 2016). The algebraic engine, the identification of the moment–SoS hierarchy with the Christoffel–Darboux kernel (Lasserre, 2021, 2023), is the same one we use for the SoS dual of the moment LP . Our reading, however, runs the sign the other way: a large λt+1(c) is bad, not good. By Proposition 3.3 it is exactly the corruption mass an adversary can hide at c from a degree-2 t certificate, so the centers a detector finds hardest to clear are precisely the centers a robust learner cannot defend. In one line, the detector’s blind spot is the adversary’s hiding place :

Hη := { c : λt+1(c) ≥ η },the set of centers at which a rate-η corruption survives every degree-2 t certificate; raising the degree is the only way to shrink it. Where that program asks “is x typical, and at what order does the empirical detector see it?”, we ask “how much malicious mass can survive at c, and at what order is it forced out?”, and we turn the answer into an impossibility statement for the certificate method, rather than a detection rule. To our knowledge the Christoffel function has served this line as a constructive tool, for detection, support and density estimation, and typicality, and has not previously been used as a barrier from which to derive learning-theoretic lower bounds; that inversion (Figure 1), and the margin–degree law it yields, is what is new here.

Remark 3.9 (Why the edge is intrinsic) . An order-t certificate is a lens, and like any lens it has a focal reach. Gauss quadrature replaces μ by a (t+1)-node measure matching its first 2 t moments, and that measure places its outermost node at Rt+1 ≍ spt; beyond it every band is swept empty. So an order-t

certificate focuses only about pt standard deviations into the tail, and a corruption parked past that edge is invisible not for want of samples but because the lens does not reach. at the same scale. Any admissible q must be ≥ 1 on the entire tail while staying

# 4 Consequences: barriers and a matching algorithm 

The framework of Section 3 has already done the work; the robust-learning statements below are its readings at specific centers. The certificate ceiling is Θ(μ(B)) inside the node range and 0 beyond it (Proposition 3.3), and each result is one corner of that dichotomy. The single resource is the degree of the Sum-of-Squares (SoS) outlier-removal certificate, the order at which the Christoffel function resolves a corruption. The reweighted-hinge approach of Shen (2025); Talwar (2020) runs on a degree-2 certificate (a variance bound on the reweighted sample); we show what raising the degree to 2 t buys, and what it does not. Theorem 4.1 reads the resolution principle off an off-center band, tying the certifiable band mass to the SoS degree through Gauss quadrature and yielding a margin–degree dichotomy that pins down where the log (1/ϵ ) in Shen’s margin comes from; Theorem 4.5 closes it to an exact law. Theorem 4.7 realizes the degree-2 corner as one explicit instance, on which degree-2 reweighting is stuck at the η1/2 rate while degree-4 escapes, exactly as λ2(c) versus λ3(c) predicts in (3.4) . Theorem 4.9 is 17 the matching algorithm: a degree-2 t program, the order-(t+1) empirical Christoffel detector, that traces the achievable frontier η1−1/2t with t = 1 recovering Shen exactly. Proposition 4.11 is the information-theoretic floor η/ 2 that the algorithm matches in rate. The two barriers (Theorems 4.1 and 4.7) are against the certificate method, not against all algorithms; we state the model each time. Throughout, the marginal is the γ-margin log-concave mixture of Section 2, ¯σ2(w) is its reweighted directional variance, and C is an absolute constant whose value may change between displays. Full proofs are deferred to Section B.

# 5 Discussion 

5.1 Context and comparisons 

The reweighted-hinge cluster, and the two limitations we explain. Hinge-loss minimization is robust because the loss is Lipschitz: a corrupted point can tilt the gradient by only so much. Talwar (2020) made this quantitative through the dense-pancake condition and a first-order (KKT) analysis, tolerating a malicious fraction η = Ω(γ) at margin γ = Ω(log d/pd). Shen (2025) sharpened the constant to η0 afixed absolute constant by reweighting the hinge: an outlier-removal program assigns weights q ∈ [0 , 1]n

and the only distributional fact it uses about the clean marginal is a degree-2 (empirical-variance) bound on the reweighted directional moment, through the controlling quantity LSN (q ◦ SD). Zeng and Shen (2025) carry the same degree-2 reweighting program into the attribute-efficient setting, inheriting the same two features, and those two features are exactly what we name. The multiclass work of Adhikari and Zeng (2026) instead removes outliers by cluster-based pruning with a standard multiclass hinge rather than by reweighting, so it lies outside the reweighted-hinge program our barriers target. Shen states only that the breakdown rate “cannot be made close to the optimal breakdown point of 12 due to inherent limitations of our approach,” without identifying the limitation, and that the margin has a “logarithmic dependence on 1 /ϵ that appears less natural,” which he conjectures “can be improved to 

γ = Ω(1/pd)” through an active-learning subroutine. We make the limitation precise: it is the degree of the certificate. Theorem 4.7 gives a clean log-concave instance on which degree 2 is provably stuck at the 

η1/2 rate that forces Shen’s small constant, while degree 4 already reaches η3/4; the constant is small because the exponent is 12 , a property of degree 2, and raising the degree lifts it toward the pancake cap (Theorem 4.9, η1−1/2t , not a march to 1 /2). The margin he calls less natural is, we show, forced :Theorem 4.1 proves the log (1/ϵ ) is conserved by every one-shot certificate of fixed degree, so it is the intrinsic price of the degree, not an artifact of his analysis, removable only by raising the degree (in 

nΩ(log 1 /ϵ ) time), enlarging the margin, or leaving the one-shot model by iterating, the very active-learning route he conjectures (Section 5.2). The companion Theorem 4.9 raises the program to degree 2 t and recovers the matching upper side η1−1/2t , so t = 1 (Shen) is the exact degree-2 corner of a frontier rather than an isolated bound. 24 The machinery we import. Degree raising is borrowed wholesale from the sum-of-squares (SoS) literature on robust statistics. The mechanism that converts a degree-2 t certified-moment bound into an outlier-removal guarantee at rate η1−1/2t is the proofs-to-algorithms paradigm of Hopkins and Li (2018) and Kothari et al. (2018), and the property that makes it apply (certifiable hypercontractivity, an SoS proof that E[( w · x)2t ] is bounded by a power of E[( w · x)2]) is due to Klivans et al. (2018), with the general subgaussian case settled by Diakonikolas et al. (2024). For us the certifiable constant is 

(C t )2t , the sub-exponential rate of a general log-concave marginal (a single Laplace coordinate already forces m2t /mt 

> 2

= ( Θ(t)) 2t , so the often-quoted (C t )t is false at this generality); this is what couples the achievable η to the SoS degree in Theorem 4.9. 

The dual direction we provably do not have. The same SoS toolkit can certify anti -concentration: Bakshi et al. (2024) give quasi-polynomial SoS certificates that a slab carries at most a prescribed mass, beyond the Gaussian case. Our dense-pancake premise needs the opposite (a degree-2 t certificate that an off-center band carries at least ρ mass), and Theorem 4.1 is precisely the statement that no such pro -concentration certificate exists below degree Ω(( |c|/s)2). This is the cone asymmetry of Section 3.1 read at the learning scale: the two faces of M2t (μ) are certifiable anti-concentration (the upper envelope) and the pro-concentration we need (the lower envelope), and only the former is low-degree. The conserved log (1/ϵ ) is that asymmetry’s price. 

What does not subsume our result. Klivans et al. (2025) achieve a 2 η + ϵ guarantee for learning halfspaces under heavy contamination by iterative filtering, an O(1) noise rate that may look like it dominates our modest constant. It does not reach our setting on four counts: it is Gaussian, not general log-concave mixture; it assumes no margin and so has no log (1/ϵ ) to conserve; it is a filtering algorithm, not a moment /SoS certificate, so the degree-cost question we study does not arise for it; and it targets agnostic (boundary-volume) error rather than the clean-inlier error err DX ( ˆw) of the malicious model. We cite it as a reference point for what is achievable absent these constraints, not as a result that absorbs the reweighted-hinge line. The agnostic and nasty-noise halfspace tradition, Diakonikolas et al. (2018) via iterative spectral filtering and the near-optimal SQ lower bounds of Diakonikolas et al. (2020), lives in a genuinely different model (OPT +ϵ error, not the margin-plus-malicious model here) and provides our methodological foil below. 

Method-specific barriers, and the SQ contrast. Our lower bounds rule out a specific class of meth-ods. Theorems 4.1 and 4.7 are barriers against the moment /SoS-certificate method , any learner whose only distributional input is the degree-≤ 2t certified moments of the clean marginal. They are not information-theoretic, and they are not statistical-query hardness. This scoping is what turns Shen’s informal “limitations of our approach” into a theorem about the approach, while leaving open that a procedure outside the model does better. The contrast with the unconditional /SQ tradition is thus between two kinds of impossibility: a SQ lower bound (Diakonikolas et al., 2020) says no efficient query algorithm of a broad class succeeds, whereas our degree barrier says no certificate of a bounded resource succeeds, and names the resource (SoS degree) whose increase provably buys the way out (traced by Theorem 4.9). The barrier and the algorithm meet at the same exponent, which is why we state them together. 25 5.2 Open problems 

• The tight breakdown η⋆ = Θ(γ/σ ). Proposition 4.11 is rate-tight ( err ≥ η/ 2) but not threshold-tight: it certifies the Θ(η) floor, not the constant at which learning fails. Under a hard margin the two-point construction provably saturates (the disagreement wedge is exponentially evacuated, 

ηsat ≤ 12 e−γ2/2σ2

), so closing the gap to the upper cap η0 = ρ/ 4 requires a multi-point or SQ band-mass argument that exploits the low boundary density ρ = Θ(γ/σ ). We conjecture the tight breakdown is η⋆ = Θ(γ/σ ).• The matching certificate-degree upper bound (now reduced to one estimate). Theorem 4.1 lower-bounds the degree of an off-center pro-concentration certificate by Ω(( |c|/s)2). Proposition 3.6 turns the matching upper bound into the weighted-Chebyshev extremal problem Et (B), and Theo-rem 4.5 makes the threshold tight, 2 t = Θ(( |c|/s)2), modulo a single estimate, Lemma 3.7: that the extremal cost stays below 1 up to the Mhaskar–Rakhmanov–Saff edge. Classical Christoffel and finite–infinite-range asymptotics (Levin and Lubinsky, 2001; Máté et al., 1991; Totik, 2000) deliver this asymptotically; deriving its uniform non-asymptotic constant is the residual open problem, and by Remark 3.9 it genuinely requires weighted potential theory rather than an elementary construction. • A self-contained general-log-concave largest-root bound. The Gauss-quadrature obstruction needs the largest orthogonal-polynomial root for the corrupted weight. We prove the two endpoints elementarily (sub-Gaussian 2 spt + 1 and sub-exponential O(st ), via Jacobi-matrix eigenvalues and Gershgorin), but the fully general log-concave case currently invokes the exponential-weight asymptotics of Levin and Lubinsky (2001). A self-contained largest- root bound for general log-concave weights would remove that black box. • Does iterative localization escape the barrier? Our barriers are scoped to one-shot certificates. Talwar (2020) and the line after it raise the possibility of an iterative localization in the style of Awasthi et al. (2017) that re-certifies on a shrinking band. Such a procedure leaves the one-shot-certificate model by construction, so Theorem 4.1 does not forbid it; whether iteration provably escapes the margin–degree barrier, and at what compute, is open and is the most likely route to removing the log (1/ϵ ) without paying nΩ(log 1 /ϵ ).• A degree-2t SoS lower bound for the joint program. By Remark 3.1, the margin–degree barrier is a degree-2 t Sum-of-Squares lower bound for the one-dimensional , per-direction band-mass certification. Lifting it to a degree-2 t SoS lower bound for the full d-dimensional reweighting program of Algorithm 1 over all directions jointly is open for t ≥ 2: the per-direction reduction is lossy (one degree-2 t pseudo-moment matrix couples all directions, and the axiswise Gauss witnesses are not jointly realizable), and the one-dimensional nonnegativity-equals-SoS exactness that powers Remark 3.1 fails in Rd . A single d-dimensional pseudo-expectation that defeats the program in every direction at once would turn the certificate barrier into a genuine SoS lower bound for the method itself. • Statistical-query hardness for the learning problem. Our barriers limit the moment /SoS-certificate method; they are not algorithm-independent. Whether margin-halfspace learning under malicious noise is hard for the broad statistical-query class (Diakonikolas et al., 2020)—a different object, needing a family of nearly-indistinguishable hard instances rather than a single certification barrier— is open, and would be the unconditional counterpart to the method-specific barriers proved here. 26 5.3 Conclusion 

Read through the degree-cost lens, robust margin-halfspace learning by reweighted hinge has a single governing quantity: the SoS degree of the outlier-removal certificate. Degree 2 is the Shen corner; degree 2t buys outlier tolerance η1−1/2t and costs nO(t) time; and two barriers fence the achievable region from below: one (Theorem 4.7) showing degree 2 cannot beat η1/2, the other (Theorem 4.1) showing any fixed degree conserves the log (1/ϵ ) margin, with the algorithm of Theorem 4.9 tracing the frontier they bound. The barriers are method-specific, against the certificate itself; making the degree the resource is what lets the algorithm and the limits be stated to the same exponent. The quantity that sets that degree is not new: it is the Christoffel function of the marginal, the same object data analysis reads to spot an outlier, here measuring instead the room an adversary has to hide one.

# E Extension to nasty noise 

The whole picture (both barriers and the degree-2 t algorithm) transfers from malicious noise to the stronger nasty-noise model of Bshouty et al. (2002), at the cost of a constant in the tolerable rate. We record the statement and the (short) modifications here. 

The model. A nasty adversary draws the n clean examples i.i.d. from D, inspects them (knowing the learner, D, and w⋆), then deletes up to ηn of them and adds up to ηn arbitrary (instance, label )

pairs, keeping the sample size n. The learner sees SC ∪ SD, where now SC is the retained clean subset (|SC | ≥ (1 − η)n) and SD the ≤ ηn added points. This subsumes malicious noise (the adversary may add without deleting) and is the sample analogue of strong contamination in robust statistics (Diakonikolas et al., 2018, 2019b); the new power is the adversary’s ability to remove clean inliers it finds inconvenient. 

Both barriers transfer a fortiori. Theorem 4.1 is a lower bound on the certificate method for certifying band mass of the clean marginal; it is a statement about μ and the SoS degree, and is unaffected by how the sample is corrupted. Theorem 4.7 is realized by a malicious instance (it adds cloaked points), which a nasty adversary can reproduce verbatim; since nasty noise subsumes malicious, the degree-2 barrier holds here as well. # F Additional related work 

The barriers in this paper sit inside a large literature on learning halfspaces under noise, much of which predates and motivates the reweighted-hinge line we analyze. We collect here, with thanks to these lines of work, the threads most relevant to our setting; the survey is necessarily partial, and we have surely omitted important contributions. This appendix expands the brief positioning of Section 5.1. 

Halfspaces under malicious and adversarial noise. The malicious-noise model is due to Kearns and Li (1993). Efficient distribution-specific learners begin with Klivans et al. (2009), whose smooth-ing /averaging analysis tolerates a noise rate scaling with the margin under isotropic log-concave marginals, and with the agnostic halfspace algorithms of Kalai et al. (2008) and Kane et al. (2013) via polynomial regression and moment matching. The localization technique of Awasthi et al. (2017) raised the tolerable malicious rate to a constant. Most directly, Shen (2021a,b, 2023, 2025) develop the reweighted-hinge and localized-perceptron approaches we study, with attribute-efficient, sparse, and multiclass extensions by Adhikari and Zeng (2026); Shen and Zhang (2021); Zeng and Shen (2025) (the last by cluster-based pruning rather than reweighting). Our degree barriers are meant to explain ,rather than improve on, the constants these works report. 

Massart, random-classification, and bounded noise. A parallel and influential line studies semi-random label noise. Diakonikolas et al. (2019a) gave the first distribution-independent halfspace learner under Massart noise, extended to general Massart noise under Gaussian marginals by Diakonikolas et al. 46 (2022b); closest in spirit to our margin-degree tradeoff, Diakonikolas et al. (2023) study the information-computation tradeoff for margin halfspaces under random classification noise. The accompanying hardness theory, statistical-query and cryptographic, is developed by Diakonikolas and Kane (2022); Diakonikolas et al. (2020, 2022c). On the active side, efficient learning under bounded, Massart, and Tsybakov noise is due to Awasthi et al. (2015, 2016); Zhang and Li (2021). 

Active and label-efficient halfspace learning. The margin-based active-learning framework of Balcan et al. (2007) and its log-concave analysis by Balcan and Long (2013), together with the perceptron-based algorithms of Yan and Zhang (2017) and the sparse, noise-tolerant active learners of Zhang (2018); Zhang et al. (2020), share our reliance on the geometry of log-concave marginals and on label-efficient outlier handling, though their resource of interest is label complexity rather than certificate degree. 

Sum-of-squares, moment methods, and robust statistics. Our algorithm imports the proofs-to-algorithms paradigm of Hopkins and Li (2018); Kothari et al. (2018), whose moment-certification machinery underlies our degree-2 t outlier removal. Closely related are the outlier-robust and list-decodable regression algorithms of Karmalkar et al. (2019); Klivans et al. (2018) and the sum-of-squares robust-estimation results of Bakshi et al. (2022); Diakonikolas et al. (2022a); attribute-efficient and list-decodable variants for sparse settings appear in Zeng and Shen (2022, 2023). These build on the foundational program of algorithmic high-dimensional robust statistics initiated by Diakonikolas et al. (2019b,c) and surveyed by Diakonikolas and Kane (2019). Our contribution is complementary: rather than a new robust algorithm, we isolate the certificate degree as the resource these methods spend, and show that spending it is, for the moment /SoS-certificate family, sometimes unavoidable. 

# G A closer look at Shen (2025) 

We collect three closer readings of Shen (2025), in each case to explain a feature rather than to fault it: why its required margin must track the target accuracy (Section G.1), the fragility of its breakdown constant (Section G.2), and a reconstruction of its reweighting program from the moment cone (Section G.3). 

G.1 The margin–accuracy coupling 

On its own terms Shen (2025) is a clean and substantial result: it is the first to learn γ-margin halfspaces under a constant malicious-noise rate for general log-concave-mixture marginals, through a single reweighted-hinge program. Our purpose here is not to dispute that achievement but to look closely at one feature of its guarantee, the margin requirement γ = Ω(log (1/ϵ )/pd), and to argue that its dependence on ϵ is a genuine conceptual wrinkle rather than a cosmetic one, before locating exactly where it comes from and how the paper proposes to pay it. 

The coupling runs backwards. A margin is a property of the distribution : it is fixed the moment the problem is posed, and the learner has no lever to enlarge it. The accuracy ϵ is a property of the learner’s 

intent : it is chosen freely, and a learner may legitimately ask for ϵ as small as it likes. A guarantee whose hypothesis γ ≥ Ω(log (1/ϵ )/pd) ties the two reads from goal to assumption rather than from assumption to conclusion: to permit a smaller target error it requires the data to have been better separated all along. Our barriers concern the exponent of the breakdown rate (the η1/2 that degree 2 forces, Theorem 4.7), not the particular constant η0 a given analysis reports. The distinction matters because the constant is fragile in a way the exponent is not, as the published degree-2 analysis already shows. Shen (2025) proves the main guarantee (his Theorem 2) for η0 ≤ 2−32 . The sampling step underlying the pruning subroutine, however (his Lemma 21, invoked through Proposition 22), is stated and proved only for η0 ∈ [ 18 , 14 ], and its proof uses the lower bound η0 ≥ 18 : the dirty-count Chernoff estimate Pr [ |¯SD| ≥ 2η0|¯S| ] ≤ exp (−η0|¯S|/3) is driven below δ by the stated sample size |¯S| ≥ 32 log (1/δ ) only because 32 ≥ 3/η 0 when η0 ≥ 18 . The gap is benign, and we record it not to dispute the theorem, which we expect holds, but because it is the symptom our framework predicts. When degree 2 squeezes the tolerable rate to a tiny constant, the modular constant-chasing that carries it through the analysis becomes correspondingly delicate: building blocks calibrated to a constant-order η0 coexist with a final bound that drives η0 far below them. Pinning the phenomenon to the certificate degree rather than to any one constant (Theorems 4.1 and 4.7) is what frees our account from this calibration: the exponent 1 − 12t is a property of the moment certificate, whatever route the constants take.

G.3 A natural reconstruction of the reweighting program 

The degree-2 program of Shen (2025) reaches its guarantee through a sequence of inequalities, each tight enough to carry the argument but none visibly forced upon us: weights q ∈ [0 , 1]n are introduced to suppress outliers, a quantity LSN (q ◦ SD) is bounded, an empirical rate ξ = 2η0 is substituted, and a chain of absolute constants is chased down to η0 ≤ 2−32 . The result is correct, but the route is the hammer and chisel: the shell is opened by force. We record here the same content read the other way. In Grothendieck’s image of the rising sea one does not crack the problem; one chooses the ambient object so large and so well-fitted that the result is already dissolved on arrival.
