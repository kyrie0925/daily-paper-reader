Abstract 

Habitat selection and space use are fundamental to understanding animal distribution and inform-ing conservation strategies. Traditional methods for quantifying habitat preferences and deriving uti-lization distributions from location data obtained from telemetry devices assume regular sampling and negligible measurement error. These assumptions are routinely violated in marine systems and prac-titioners typically regularize and filter data before fitting models. These two-step procedures fail to propagate uncertainty from the filtering stage into subsequent analyses and can yield biased estimates. Habitat-driven Langevin diffusion models offer an elegant alternative by representing movement as a stochastic process driven by habitat-selection dynamics in continuous time, naturally accommodat-ing irregular sampling. However, a state-space formulation that incorporates measurement error by treating true locations as latent variables remains challenging because habitat data depending on these locations are no longer observed. We facilitate the use of such state-space formulation by using the Laplace approximation to simultaneously integrate over the true locations and account for the habi-tat covariates encountered along those latent paths, yielding a single-stage framework that can be ef-ficiently implemented with Template Model Builder (TMB). By doing so, we provide the first TMB implementation capable of handling covariates that depend on latent variables, allowing inference via fast and efficient maximum likelihood estimation. Simulation studies demonstrated our approach out-performs the two-step method, recovering the sign and overall magnitude of habitat-selection parame-ters even under substantial measurement error and missing data (both common in marine studies) and yielding more accurate utilization distribution and trajectory reconstructions. An application to nar-whal telemetry data demonstrates that the two-step method masks the habitat selection coefficient substantially towards zero selection, while our unified approach recovers a much stronger selection signal. By providing a solution for both measurement error and temporal irregularity when inferring habitat selection and utilization distributions, our approach addresses several long-standing challenges and offers a computationally efficient, flexible framework for movement ecologists that is applicable across a wide range of taxa and environments. 

# 1 Introduction 

Animals do not move uniformly across their habitat, and mapping the probability of species presence across a landscape is fundamental in ecology, and for informing conservation and ecosystem management (Cagnacci et al., 2010; Northrup et al., 2022; Matthiopoulos et al., 2023). Habitat selection analyses aim to uncover these dynamics by quantifying which environmental features animals select relative to what is available to them, thereby indicating relative preference (or avoidance) for particular habitats (Manly et al., 2002; Matthiopoulos et al., 2023). This idea underlies the concept of the utilization distribution (UD; Anderson, 11982), which provides a spatial map of relative intensity of space use, showing where the animal is most likely to be. The UD thus helps identifying home ranges (Sprogis et al., 2016), key habitats (Hebblewhite and Merrill, 2009), migration corridors (Sawyer et al., 2009), and zones of potential overlap with human activities (Halliday et al., 2021). Such insights are vital for better understanding animal space use and guiding conservation and management efforts (McWhinnie et al., 2018). Advances in tracking technology have allowed ecologists to collect extensive fine-scale movement data for many species across diverse en-vironments (Patterson et al., 2017; Nathan et al., 2022). Movement data are rich multivariate time series that can offer insights into how animals respond to environmental conditions (Hebblewhite and Merrill, 2009), disturbance (e.g., presence of predator; Fortin et al., 2005), and social factors (Strandburg-Peshkin et al., 2017), thereby helping us understand patterns of space use. Many methods have been developed to estimate habitat selection and UDs from telemetry data. Standard approaches fit a resource selection function (RSF) to quantify habitat selection (Boyce and McDonald, 1999; Boyce et al., 2002; Manly et al., 2002; Johnson et al., 2013; Hooten et al., 2017; Hazen et al., 2021; Matthiopoulos et al., 2023), and the UD is then derived as the normalized product of the RSF and an availability distribution, which describes the distribution of habitat covariates over locations accessible to the animal (Hooten et al., 2017; Matthiopou-los et al., 2023). Although multiple methods exist for fitting RSFs to telemetry data, the most common are use–available logistic regression (Freitas et al., 2016; Florko et al., 2023) and point process models (Warton and Shepherd, 2010; Johnson et al., 2013; Hooten et al., 2017). Despite their widespread use, RSFs rely on the assumption of spatio-temporal independence among locations, which is increasingly vi-olated by modern high-resolution movement data (Patterson et al., 2017). Statistical methods have there-fore been developed to model how animals respond to environmental conditions while accounting for the inherent spatio-temporal structure of movement data. Although several approaches have been proposed to account for autocorrelation in RSFs, such as data thinning (Northrup et al., 2013), weighted likelihood (Alston et al., 2023), generalized estimating equa-tions (Prima et al., 2017), and variance inflation (Nielsen et al., 2002), step-selection functions (SSFs) have emerged as a prominent solution by conditioning habitat selection on the animal’s current location and movement capacity (Forester et al., 2009; Thurfjell et al., 2014; Avgar et al., 2016; Fieberg et al., 2021; Florko et al., 2025). Specifically, at each step, habitat availability is constrained by the animal’s move-ment, such that selection is evaluated only among locations that are reachable given the preceding posi-tion and movement characteristics. Step-selection functions therefore describe habitat selection at a local scale, and the parameter estimates are inherently tied to the temporal resolution of the sampled obser-vations (Schl¨ agel and Lewis, 2016). While some ecological questions are precisely focused on such local, 2short-term selection, for example, how the immediate presence of a predator affects prey habitat selection (Matthews et al., 2020), this dependence is limiting in the many ecological applications that focus on char-acterizing the species’ broad scale space use, which is more naturally described by a spatial UD (Michelot et al., 2019). Although step-scale selection can be translated into long-term space use, both theoretical and empirical work have shown that SSFs do not recover the UD implied by an RSF (Signer et al., 2017; Michelot et al., 2019). Moreover, since parameter estimates depend directly on the sampling interval, SSF-derived inferences are not directly comparable across datasets collected at different temporal resolutions, nor easily related to broader-scale species distribution models. Several approaches have been proposed to upscale inference from SSFs to the UD (see Potts and B¨ orger, 2023 for a review), though the most commonly used rely on simulation-based procedures (Signer et al., 2024) rather than explicit theoretical formulations. This reliance on simulation makes it difficult to obtain a simple parametric representation of the UD and complicates the quantification of uncertainty, which is typically ignored (Michelot and Hanks, 2024). Continuous-time stochastic processes have recently provided an elegant solution to these issues, naturally accommodating irregularly sampled data while yielding a global UD from local movement decisions via their limiting distribution (e.g., Calabrese et al., 2016; Scharf et al., 2017; Wilson et al., 2018; Michelot et al., 2019). Among them, the habitat-driven Langevin diffu-sion (Michelot et al., 2019; Michelot and Hanks, 2024) has emerged as a particularly promising approach for habitat selection and UD inference across temporal scales. At the fine scale, the Langevin stochastic differential equation models continuous movement dynamics, with a drift driven by local gradients in the habitat potential surface. This potential surface is defined by a standard RSF, ensuring that the station-ary distribution of the location process (i.e., the UD) is strictly proportional to the RSF. This broad-scale UD emerges from continuous, fine-scale movement responses to these gradients over time. In recent years, growing interest in the Langevin model has led to multiple extensions (McClintock and Lander, 2024; Del-porte et al., 2025). Standard habitat selection models, including the Langevin diffusion, do not account for location measure-ment error. However, such error is pervasive in animal telemetry studies, particularly in marine systems (Costa et al., 2010; Service Argos, 2017; Wildlife Computers Inc., 2024). Commonly used tracking data that are subject to measurement error include the Advanced Research and Global Observation Satellite (Argos; Service Argos, 2017) and Fastloc GPS systems (Bryant, 2007; Costa et al., 2010). Inferring habi-tat selection from noisy locations can result in bias and, ultimately, misleading conclusions. To address this issue, state-space models (SSMs) are routinely used as a preliminary filtering step. Implemented via 

R packages such as aniMotum (Jonsen et al., 2020), ctmm (Calabrese et al., 2016) and crawl (Johnson and 3London, 2018; Jonsen et al., 2020), these models incorporate measurement error and aim to predict the true movement track, typically modeled as a random walk. This two-step approach, which first corrects for measurement error and then performs ecological inference using the corrected locations, has become a standard analysis pipeline in habitat selection analyses of error-prone data (e.g., Matthews et al., 2020; Martin et al., 2024; Florko et al., 2025). However, most users do not propagate the uncertainty from the filtering stage into subsequent analyses, which may therefore introduce bias, the extent of which has not been thoroughly investigated (Michelot et al., 2019). Such bias may arise, for example, when the move-ment model used for filtering is overly simplistic. Building on the Langevin diffusion framework, we explicitly express the stationary distribution as an RSF and incorporate measurement error into the model via an SSM. We propose a highly efficient likelihood-based fitting method implemented within the Template Model Builder (TMB; Kristensen et al., 2016) framework. By combining automatic differentiation with the Laplace approximation, TMB enables effi-cient and scalable maximum likelihood inference in complex SSMs, while simultaneously recovering un-observed true trajectories. Crucially, our setting differs from standard state-space TMB implementations (e.g., Auger-M´ eth´ e et al., 2017) because the habitat covariates must be evaluated at the unobserved true locations. Specifically, we account for the unobserved covariate values while integrating over the true lo-cations during the optimization process within TMB. To our knowledge, this is the first TMB implemen-tation of SSMs to handle covariates that depend on latent variables, resolving a fundamental challenge in ecology. The proposed approach is therefore fast, intuitive, and powerful, making it well-suited for esti-mating habitat-selection parameters and UDs from complex tracking data. We evaluate our method’s ability to recover the UD and habitat selection parameters in a simulation study spanning a wide range of measurement-error and missing-data scenarios. We also compare its performance with that of a standard two-step approach. We then illustrate our approach with tracking data from nar-whal ( Monodon monoceros ), a species vulnerable to the effects of climate change such as declines in sea ice and rising shipping traffic (Pizzolato et al., 2014; Halliday et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that bathymetry plays a significant role in narwhal habitat selection (Laidre et al., 2004; Kenyon et al., 2018; Hornby et al., 2025). In this context, our objective is to assess our method’s ability to identify this relationship and to compare it with the results from a two-step approach. 42 Methods 

## Langevin diffusion model for animal movement 

Movement, represented as the joint velocity and location process, is modeled as a underdamped Langevin diffusion process (Michelot et al., 2019; Michelot and Hanks, 2024), which generalizes correlated random walks by allowing movement to be directed towards regions of higher values of a potential function de-noted as log π. The stationary distribution π represents the (time-invariant) steady-state distribution of the location process. In habitat selection analyses, π is typically written as a function of an RSF and cor-responds to a UD (Manly et al., 2002; Michelot et al., 2019). Local movement decisions in the Langevin framework reflect habitat selection preferences (drift towards higher log π), with resource availability dy-namically constrained by the movement process itself. Since π is the limiting distribution of the location process, the animal’s long-term pattern of space use stabilizes to π, creating a direct link between local habitat selection and broad-scale space use. This model is referred to as the Langevin movement model throughout. Let μt = ( μxt , μ yt )⊤ and vt = ( vxt , v yt )⊤ denote the location and velocity in the x and y directions at time t ≥ 0. ## Measurement model for error-prone observed location data 

The Langevin movement model traditionally assumes that locations are observed without error. However, most tracking data are recorded with some degree of uncertainty. For example, Argos data are typically accompanied by error estimates derived from least-squares or Kalman filtering, while Fastloc GPS data are characterized by circular error (McClintock, 2017; Service Argos, 2017; Wildlife Computers Inc., 2024). To address this, we couple the Langevin movement model with an observation model that accounts for mea-surement error, specifically for Kalman-filtered Argos and Fastloc GPS data (Jonsen et al., 2020). The core idea is that the observed locations correspond to the true locations with additive noise, the magni-tude and distribution of which depends on the type of data and the associated uncertainty estimates. Fol-lowing standard practice in the animal movement literature, we use a Gaussian observation model (Mc-Clintock et al., 2015; Jonsen et al., 2020). ## 2.1 Inference 

As both the true positions μi and the velocities vi are not observed, they need to be integrated out of the joint likelihood function. Crucially, this also means the spatial covariates, which depend on the latent posi-tions, are not observed. We account for this covariate uncertainty by extracting the covariate values ck(μ)and calculating the gradients ∇ck(μ) from raster data during the optimization within TMB (Kristensen et al., 2016), a technique that, to our knowledge, has not been previously used in this framework. Because the spatial covariates are a function of the latent locations, they are integrated over all possible true paths via the Laplace approximation. We conduct inference by maximizing the marginal likelihood function of the observed locations under the Langevin movement model. An attrac-tive candidate for numerically efficient approximate frequentist inference is given by the Laplace approx-imation (Erkanli, 1994; Van der Vaart, 2000). The Laplace method approximates the log-integrand by a second-order Taylor expansion (with respect to z) centered at its mode, for a given parameter vector θ.Plugging this approximation back into equation (13) results in a Gaussian integral with an explicit ana-lytical solution. Conveniently, the approach is fully automated and readily available in TMB (Kristensen et al., 2016), provided the user defines the joint negative log likelihood (see Appendix S1: Theoretical background on the Laplace approximation but also see Additional implementation details on the Laplace approximation). Once the model is fitted, TMB provides model parameter estimates and, conveniently, predictions of the random effects (true locations and velocity in our case) as well as uncertainties for both, respectively. The package is numerically highly efficient by using automatic differentiation to compute first and second derivatives required for the Laplace approximation, as well as by automatically detecting spar-sity in the second derivative function. The Laplace approximation is exact when the state-space model is linear and Gaussian, such that the joint distribution of all random effects is multivariate Gaussian (Tierney and Kadane, 1986). Here, the non-linearity introduced by πUD (·) in equation (5) violates this condition, hence in our case, the procedure is approximate. Nevertheless, the Laplace approximation often performs remarkably well in practice, partic-ularly when the integrand fθ (y, z) is dominated by a single mode and the sample size provides sufficient information to concentrate the distribution (Tierney and Kadane (1986); further theoretical details on the Laplace approximation, including potential sources of bias and implementation details, are provided in Ap-pendix Appendix S1: Theoretical background on the Laplace approximation and Additional implementa-tion details on the Laplace approximation). Intuitively, a higher BA indicates that the two UDs assign similar probabilities of use to the same locations, meaning that an animal simulated from the true UD and one simulated from the estimated UD would be likely to be found in the same areas. The procedure requires initial values for both the model parameters θ and the latent process z (true loca-tions and velocities), as estimation is carried out by jointly maximizing the likelihood over parameters and the latent process. In particular, since observed locations are treated as noisy measurements of the true positions, the true location trajectory is latent. Since complex models can be sensitive to initialization in likelihood-based optimization (Auger-M´ eth´ e et al., 2016), we adopt a strategy informed by preliminary simulations. Latent locations are initialized at the observed positions, with missing values filled by linear interpolation separately for each track, and velocities are initialized at zero. For the model parameters, we consider two approaches: a neutral initialization, where γ and σ are estimated from the data and all selec-tion coefficients are set to zero, and an initialization at the true parameter values. To improve numerical stability, an initial optimization over the latent states is performed conditional on fixed model parameters before proceeding to joint optimization over both states and parameters. For the two-step method, we first apply a pre-filtering step, fitting a correlated random walk with aniMotum 

as is standard in ecological studies (e.g., Florko et al., 2025). In the second step, we then fit the Langevin movement model, treating the predicted locations from the correlated random walk as the “true” loca-tions. When data are missing, aniMotum provides predicted locations at both observed and missing time points, effectively imputing the missing data such that the two-step method is fitted on a complete se-quence of predicted locations. Model fitting is performed using TMB.

## 3.1 Simulation study 

The Langevin SSM outperforms the two-step method in estimating parameters for both movement and habitat selection (Figure 2). The Langevin SSM consistently maintains a high Bhattacharyya’s affinity (BA ≈ 1), demonstrating its strong performance in preserving space-use information, even with large mea-surement error in the location data. In all scenarios, the Langevin SSM yields substantially less biased es-timates of habitat selection parameters, whereas the two-step approach systematically underestimates the strength of habitat selection. The Langevin SSM recovers trajectories nearly identical to the true (simu-lated) ones, whereas the two-step method still carries much of the added noise (Figure S2). This residual noise also impacts the underlying movement-habitat relationship, leading the two-step approach to inflate estimates of the speed parameter ( γ) (Figure S3). 16 Measurement error is the main driver of attenuation bias in both methods (Figures 2a-c). # 4 Discussion 

We proposed a Langevin state-space model to infer animal habitat selection and utilization distributions from irregular, error-prone, and complex movement data. Maximum likelihood inference is carried out ef-ficiently using the Laplace approximation and automatic differentiation as implemented in TMB, with an R package for simulating and fitting the model available on GitHub ( https://github.com/bmcclintock/ langevinSSM ). The proposed method greatly improves upon the traditional two-step approach, which con-sists of first fitting a simpler model to account for measurement error and/or temporal irregularity and then fitting a more complex model to the predicted locations from the first stage. Our simulations showed that the Langevin SSM better preserves the UD, minimizes bias in habitat selection parameters, and im-proves the recovery of individual trajectories. When applied to narwhal data, our method preserves more of the information contained in the raw observations than the two-step approach, enabling the recovery of a stronger effect of bathymetry. Narwhal tracking data are highly irregular, combine multiple data sources (Fastloc GPS and Argos), and are collected in complex landscapes such as fjord systems. The strong per-formance of our method reflects its suitability for accommodating these data complexities. As measurement error increases, we found that both methods tend to underestimate the strength of habi-tat selection, with parameter estimates increasingly biased towards zero selection. This bias reflects a loss of information arising from greater uncertainty, leading to a weaker habitat-selection signal. However, this bias is consistently larger and more substantial for the two-step method, even with low measurement er-ror, whereas it grows only gradually with the Langevin SSM. This discrepancy in bias suggests that two-step approaches are not well suited to accommodate substantial measurement error. By remaining robust to such uncertainty, our method provides a more reliable framework for estimating utilization distribu-tions and associated habitat-selection parameters. Both methods appeared robust to missing data, likely because they are formulated in continuous time and evaluated on a fine temporal grid, such that spo-21 radic removals of observations have only a negligible impact on inference. Although we examined a broad range of scenarios to represent realistic animal movement patterns, our analysis was not exhaustive and future research should investigate the effect of introducing missing data in blocks. Additionally, the effect of sampling rate and covariate correlation on the performance of the proposed method should be explored. Coarser sampling intervals may introduce bias (e.g., Michelot et al. 2019; McClintock and Lander 2024; Michelot and Hanks 2024, but see Blackwell and Matthiopoulos 2024 for an approach to mitigating such attenuation bias), while correlated covariates can reduce inferential power. Fortunately, the speed with which our Langevin SSM can be fitted with TMB facilitates further simulation studies investigating the properties of the model. For example, simulations with n = 25 ,000 observations on average required only 14 sec per model fit on an Apple M2 Max with 64GB RAM. Extensive simulation studies for the Langevin SSM simply are not practical using sampling-based approaches (e.g., Markov chain Monte Carlo, particle filtering). The relatively poor performance of the two-step method was not surprising because the true data-generating model was a Langevin diffusion, whereas the movement model fitted in the first step of the two-step ap-proach was a correlated random walk that ignored habitat selection. However, it is common practice in movement ecology to use a simpler model in the first step to filter and regularize raw tracking data, and subsequently fit a more complex model to the processed tracks for habitat selection analysis. The two-step approach could in principle be improved by using a more flexible movement model in the first step, such as a move persistence model (Auger-M´ eth´ e et al., 2017; Jonsen et al., 2020), which extends the correlated random walk by allowing behavioral persistence to vary over time. However, we found that even the more modest upgrade from a simple random walk to a correlated random walk did not greatly improve model performance, with comparable levels of bias in the selection coefficients and inflation in γ and σ (see Ap-pendix S1: Additional results). Besides, more complex models are often harder to fit. Together, these re-sults suggest that the core limitation of the two-step approach lies not in the choice of movement model but in the decoupling of movement from habitat selection. Since habitat selection inherently influences movement, estimating movement independently in a first step necessarily introduces bias. Jointly estimat-ing both, as in the Langevin SSM, is therefore inherently better suited to account for measurement error and temporal irregularity in the data. Our findings from the narwhal movement data align with previous works identifying bathymetry as an important covariate in narwhal habitat selection (Laidre et al., 2004; Kenyon et al., 2018; Hornby et al., 2025). The relatively low persistence velocity estimate of approximately 15 minutes is likely due to the complex geography of the area. The intricate fjord system requires frequent directional changes, which 22 prevents narwhal from maintaining straight trajectories and limits the model’s ability to detect prolonged periods of persistence in movement. Despite these frequent directional changes, our results revealed a clear pattern in depth preference and suggest an increased use of deep-water habitats. These findings are consis-tent with previous studies (Richard et al., 1994; Nunavut Planning Commission (NPC), 2000; Watt et al., 2017), and align with Watt et al. (2017) who estimated a summer kernel utilization distribution focused along the deep central section of Eclipse Sound. As deep-diving marine mammals, narwhal likely use these areas primarily for foraging (Watt and Ferguson, 2015; Watt et al., 2017). However, deep diving may also be associated with other behaviors. For example, bottom contact during dives could help molting (Watt et al., 2017), or alternatively deep diving may reflect a behavioral response to disturbance (Dupont et al., 2026). Furthermore, the association between deep-water habitat use and deep-diving should be interpreted with caution, as narwhal may also spend considerable time at shallow depths within deep-water areas, such as during social interactions (Watt et al., 2017; Hornby et al., 2025). It is also important to note that our model assumes a linear relationship between bathymetry and habitat selection, which may not fully capture the complexity of narwhal space use. The true relationship could be non-linear, with narwhal selecting for a particular depth range rather than responding uniformly across the bathymetric gradient. Moreover, additional environmental covariates such as distance to shore and seafloor slope are expected to influence habitat selection (Hornby et al., 2025). After developing our approach, we became aware of the related work by Delporte et al. (2025), who inde-pendently incorporate measurement error into the Langevin framework via an SSM. However, their for-mulation does not express the stationary distribution in terms of habitat covariates, and their focus and methods differ considerably from ours. Specifically, Delporte et al. (2025) propose a Langevin movement model with a Gaussian mixture potential surface that explicitly incorporates measurement error to esti-mate true tracks from noisy data. Their approach models observations as Student’s t deviations around the true locations, using a mixture of t-distributions to accommodate both heavy-tailed and X-shaped error structures. Particle filtering is then used to recover the underlying trajectories given the model pa-rameters. While their method effectively reconstructs true trajectories from noisy observations, it assumes model parameters are known and does not use the Langevin movement model for habitat selection infer-ence, leaving both parameter estimation and habitat selection inference unexplored. Building on the same modeling framework, we explicitly express the stationary distribution as a resource selection function and simultaneously estimate all model parameters and unobserved processes via maximum likelihood. To re-duce the computational costs of particle filtering (Doucet and Johansen, 2011), we opt for a TMB Laplace approximation, offering a faster and more scalable solution. 23 Adding a penalty term to the negative log-likelihood discouraged locations from being estimated on land and substantially improved location estimation compared with the two-step approach. The method pro-posed by Delporte et al. (2025), which enforces spatial constraints directly within the stochastic differ-ential equation, is closely related to our approach. Specifically, their (exact) formulation can be approxi-mated by including d2water as a covariate in π(μ), where βwater = 1 /2λ and λ is the penalty term of Del-porte et al. (2025). Differences in performance between the two approaches are likely attributable to differ-ent modeling choices. In particular, Delporte et al. (2025) use a potential surface with attraction towards activity centers, which further pulls locations towards the center of the feasible domain and may help keep them away from land. However, such a hotspot may not be ecologically appropriate for narwhal in Qikiq-taaluk. Additionally, their simulation study is conducted with known model parameters, thereby remov-ing an additional source of variability. The rotational component in their SDE, however, is a particularly promising extension for species moving through spatially constrained environments such as narwhal in nar-row fjords Delporte et al. (2025). We did not explicitly address the potential bias that can be introduced by the discretization in equation (4), which assumes that ∇ log π(μ) remains constant along the movement path between consecutive obser-vations at times ti and ti+1 . This bias should not be an issue in our simulation study, since we use a very fine temporal grid to minimize it, but it may be problematic in applications to real data with irregular and sometimes large observation gaps. In practice, the magnitude of this bias is difficult to assess since it de-pends jointly on the speed of the animal’s movement, the strength of the drift term, and the rate at which environmental covariates change across space. As such, we recommend that practitioners working with coarser temporal resolutions (e.g., a couple of observations a day) consider the method proposed by Black-well and Matthiopoulos (2024) to reduce this discretization bias, which can be readily incorporated within our framework, though it comes at an increased computational cost. Future work could explore whether the trade-off is worthwhile for improving parameter estimates in practical applications. An alternative so-lution is provided by Delporte et al. (2025), who directly tackle this limitation using splitting schemes, which split the non-linear SDE into a linear part with an exact Gaussian solution and a non-linear ordi-nary differential equation solved separately. Their approach elegantly handles non-linearity, although it would also likely increase computational cost. The Langevin movement model provides a natural and powerful framework for quantifying habitat selec-tion and deriving utilization distributions from spatial covariates. We focused on bathymetry in our nar-whal example, although numerous other spatial features are relevant in marine systems, such as foraging “hotspots”, distance to colony, or distance to shore (e.g., Auger-M´ eth´ e et al., 2025). Habitat selection 24 analyses using the Langevin movement model typically do not account for the temporal dynamics inher-ent in many environmental covariates (e.g., sea-ice concentration, temperature). Allowing covariates to vary over time violates the stationarity assumption of the Langevin movement model defined in equation (1). Consequently, the resulting potential surface represents instantaneous habitat selection rather than a true long-term utilization distribution, but this can still provide valuable insights into animal movement patterns (Michelot and Hanks, 2024). One simple approach to address this limitation is to incorporate av-erage covariate values over the course of a study, as is frequently done in habitat selection studies (e.g., sea surface temperature in Johnson et al. (2013), shipping traffic density in Wynn-Simmonds et al., 2025). Ex-ploring formulations with time-varying covariates while preserving some form of stationarity (e.g., period-ically varying covariates as shown by Koslik et al., 2025 in hidden Markov models) would be a promising direction for future work. Time-varying habitat-selection coefficients that allow space use to change over time (e.g., by season Chambault et al., 2018; Michelot and Hanks, 2024) is another promising avenue for future research.

# B Theoretical background on the Laplace approximation 

In this section, we provide further details on the theoretical foundations of the Laplace approximation and discuss potential sources of bias. The Laplace approximation assumes that the integrand fθ (y, z), viewed as a function of z, is well approx-imated by a Gaussian centred at its mode (Tierney and Kadane, 1986). Since gψ (y | z) is Gaussian in z

by equation (8), the only source of non-Gaussianity in the integrand is the density hβ,σ,γ (z). When the transition function between consecutive latent variables is Gaussian and linear in z, the integrand is ex-34 actly Gaussian and the Laplace approximation is exact (Tierney and Kadane, 1986). However, the non-linearity introduced by π(·) in equation (4) renders the integrand non-Gaussian in z, which can intro-duce bias in the approximated marginal likelihood. While the approximation generally fails when the in-tegrand has multiple modes, a Gaussian approximation can remain appropriate even when the integrand is non-Gaussian, provided it is unimodal and well-concentrated around its mode (Bruijn, 1961; Tierney and Kadane, 1986). An additional theoretical consideration is that standard asymptotic justifications for the Laplace approx-imation do not apply when the number of random effects grows with the sample size (Rue et al., 2009), as is the case here since both locations and velocities are treated as latent variables. However, the Markov structure implies that each latent state zi depends only on its neighbors and its associated observation 

yi, such that the approximation relies on the local rather than global behavior of the integrand. Thus the Laplace approximation is expected to perform well when time intervals ∆ i are short, such that ∇ log π

varies little between consecutive observations and the transition density between consecutive latent states is close to Gaussian. We therefore recommend limiting large time gaps in the data and performing track segmentation when necessary (e.g., as described in Section 2.3). 

# C Additional implementation details on the Laplace approxima-tion 

Conveniently, the Laplace approximation is fully automated in the TMB R package (Kristensen et al., 2016), requiring only that the user provides the negative log-integrand from equation (13) as a C++ script. The package then returns the (negative) logarithm of the Laplace approximation to equation (13) along with its gradient as a standard R function, which can be directly used for numerical optimization with quasi-Newton methods. During each optimization step—i.e., each call to the function or its gradient—TMB automatically performs an inner optimization over z, required for the Taylor expansion in the Laplace approximation. For users familiar with C++ , TMB provides a flexible framework to implement fast, auto-matic Laplace approximations for a wide range of models, requiring only the specification of the negative log-likelihood (Kristensen et al., 2016; Auger-M´ eth´ e et al., 2017; Auger-M´ eth´ e et al., 2021; McClintock, 2021; Breivik et al., 2025). 35 D Additional details on the Laplace approximation in the nar-whal case study

## Spatial constraint in the Langevin SSM 

We conducted a simulation study to assess whether habitat selection patterns are preserved and accurately recovered when a spatial constraint is incorporated into the model as in equation (16). We use the same framework as in the main simulation study, with an additional covariate consisting of fourteen polygons of varying sizes representing landmasses, together with the boundary of the study area buffered by 20 units to represent the surrounding coastline, to which a strongly negative selection coefficient ( −100) was as-signed to enforce strict avoidance. Movement was then simulated under high measurement error (50% and 60%) to ensure that a proportion of observed locations fell within the constrained regions. During esti-mation, a negative selection coefficient was included for the constraint covariate in π in addition to the penalty written as a function of d2water in equation (16), with ψ fixed at its true value. We additionally examined the effect of placing a prior on σ. However, the UD was computed without the constraint covari-ate, as it represents a hard spatial constraint rather than a genuine avoidance process, yielding the same UD as in the previous simulation study. Figure S5 illustrates examples of the constrained UD and simu-lated tracks under 60% measurement error (note that the figure displays the UD and observed locations only), where the dark patches represent constrained regions. 47 The introduction of a spatial constraint leads to an increase in bias and standard deviations in parameter estimates compared with the unconstrained Langevin SSM. Nevertheless, this bias remains substantially lower than that of the two-step method without spatial constraints. Under 60% measurement error, the two-step approach exhibits considerably larger bias (2 .96, −4.37, and 2 .66) than the constrained Langevin SSM (1 .6, −2.27 and −1.96). An increase in standard deviation is also observed, with values of 1 .91, 2 .59, and 2 .22 for β1, β2, and β3 under 50% error, and 1 .97, 2 .66, and 2 .29 under 60% error. The increase in variance likely reflects the additional complexity introduced by the spatial constraint: when observations fall on land, latent locations must be relocated to water, leading to greater variability in the estimation of habitat selection parameters as the model reconciles these invalid locations with the stationary distri-bution covariate. However, the average BA is lower for the two-step approach (0 .72 and 0 .71) compared with the constrained Langevin SSM (0 .84 and 0 .82). Therefore, our method is able to accommodate spa-tial constraints while maintaining a higher BA and lower bias than the two-step approach. A number of simulations produced unusually large estimates of γ and σ (≥ 100, not represented in Fig-ure S7 for display purposes; note that when one parameter explodes the other does too). We believe this is because when observations with large measurement error fall on land, the constraint forces the latent lo-cations to be relocated towards water and, in some cases, this relocation is accommodated by inflating the movement speed, leading to larger estimates of σ and a corresponding increase in γ to preserve the habi-tat selection patterns (Michelot and Hanks, 2024). This phenomenon is observed in approximately 30% of simulations with 50% measurement error and 34% with 60% measurement error (Figure S7). This behav-ior also occurred in the narwhal case study, motivating our interest in addressing it. Adding an explicit penalty on the speed parameter improves the results. The bias in the selection parame-ters decreased, with values of 0 .32, −0.32, and −0.27 for β1, β2, and β3, respectively, under 50% measure-ment error and of 0 .39, −0.36, and −0.30 under 60% measurement error. No simulations produced explod-ing ( ≥ 100) movement parameters. The decrease in bias was accompanied by an increase in average BA across simulated datasets from 0 .84 to 0 .94 with 50% error and from 0 .82 to 0 .94 with 60% error. We compared the constrained Langevin SSM to the standard two-step approach without any spatial con-straint, rather than to a version that removes on-land locations before fitting, as is often done in ecological studies (Auger-M´ eth´ e et al., 2025). Some other preprocessing approaches exist, such as rerouting locations around land (e.g., path rerouting function in aniMotum ), but we did not explore these alternatives. This reflects a key goal of our work: to avoid pre-filtering the data and retain as much information as possible. The main objective of this simulation study is to evaluate how the penalty influences the Langevin SSM 48 estimates. Even when compared with the unconstrained two-step method, the constrained Langevin SSM consistently outperforms the two-step approach.
